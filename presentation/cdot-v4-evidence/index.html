C-DOT · 5G UPF traffic steering · evidence review

Steering Under Notice

Predictive UPF steering beats the static controller — but only when the capacity loss is declared in advance. On 5,000 paired simulations using seeds that played no part in choosing the configuration, guarded pre-drain removed 55% of uplink overload on scheduled maintenance and never once lost a single case.

The result

Two operating points, not one recommendation

The campaign screened 160 controller configurations, then re-tested the survivors on a fresh seed pool that had never been inspected. Three of four candidates held every acceptance gate. They are not competitors — they are the two ends of a risk-and-reward choice that C-DOT gets to make.

The trade is legible.

Pre-drain buys roughly half again as much overload reduction. MPC buys an order of magnitude less collateral harm (0.016 against 0.179) and a tenth the routing churn — at the cost of decisions that take 38 seconds instead of under a second.

The honest shape of it

Wins, ties, and one place it can lose

A claim that predictive steering is simply better than static would not survive scrutiny, and it is not what the data says. The result has three states, and the third one matters most in a review.

Declared capacity loss

473 informative pairs · 0 regressions

Scheduled maintenance, and maintenance followed by a stadium-scale demand surge. Median case removes 72% and 67% of uplink overload respectively.

Not one pair out of 473 came out worse than static.

Pure surprise

500 pairs · bit-exact ties

An unannounced brownout, or an unannounced demand surge. With no declared event the controller publishes exactly the static policy.

This is designed behaviour, and it is verified byte-for-byte rather than assumed.

Declared loss, then a surprise

243 informative pairs · 74 regressions (30.5%)

Maintenance is announced, the controller drains traffic onto healthy UPFs — and then one of those browns out without warning.

Still net positive (+12.8% in aggregate, median case +20.5%), but the worst cases are bad. MPC cuts this to 6.6% of pairs.

The operational finding

Advance notice is the resource that matters

Gain scales with how much warning the maintenance window carries — and it keeps scaling out to four hours. Thirty minutes of notice is worth a quarter of what four hours is worth. This is a scheduling decision C-DOT already controls.

Faster control does not substitute for earlier warning

Running the controller every 2 minutes instead of every 10 was tested across all 160 configurations. For pre-drain it delivers identical benefit — 7.8% mean, 27.7% best, matching 10-minute to two decimal places — at 3.4× the routing churn, which is why only 8 of 64 pass instead of 26. For MPC, holding the optimisation to the same size means faster cadence buys less lookahead, and the benefit halves. Both point the same way: with compute held constant, the binding resource is forecast lookahead, not decision frequency.

Why this supersedes the earlier review

The previous campaign measured its own evaluator

An earlier 160-configuration campaign concluded that no controller could beat static and that static should remain the recommendation. That conclusion was an artifact. Three defects sat in the scenario generator and the scoring code, not in the controllers.

What was actually wrong

The scored metric and the optimised metric disagreed. The simulator scores overload as load ÷ capacity − 1, a relative quantity. The safety guard and the pre-drain solver both worked in absolute Mbps. On a UPF cut to a tenth of its envelope those differ tenfold, so the controller systematically undervalued the exact action it exists to take. Correcting the units turned the worst family from −1.7% to positive.

One stress family produced infinite overload. An unavailable UPF has zero capacity, so every such pair scored infinity for both controllers — an exact tie that nonetheless failed a finite-metric gate on all 160 configurations. That single gate made the contract unpassable before any controller ran.

The MPC horizon was sized in windows, not hours. Halving the cadence quintupled the optimisation, and every 2-minute configuration hit a 2-second solver budget that the acceptance contract allowed to be 120 seconds. The reported cadence finding was measuring a sizing bug.

Method

Selected on one set of seeds, judged on another

Configurations that look good on the data that chose them are not evidence. The campaign ran in two stages: 160 configurations screened on a development seed pool, then the survivors frozen and re-run on a validation pool that was never inspected beforehand. The selection rule was written down first, and ranks on the lower confidence bound rather than the headline number.

Shrinkage of three to four points is what honest bias removal looks like. A candidate that collapsed here would have been a screening artifact; none did. Validation seeds are disjoint from the screening pool, from every earlier campaign, and from the protected seed set.

What every passing configuration had to clear

Mean gain at or above 10% across all five families; a bootstrap 95% lower bound above zero; no aggregate regression in any family; overload added no more than a quarter of overload removed; no regression in downlink overload, drops, rejections or session establishment; no solver timeout or error; routing churn within 0.30 per group-hour; decisions inside 120 seconds; and bit-exact static behaviour whenever no event is declared.

Limits

What this evidence does not establish

  • This is a shadow controller in simulation, not a production result. No policy was applied to live traffic. The simulator models new-session placement only — roughly 3% of offered load is controllable, because established sessions are never migrated.
  • One stress family produced no measurable stress. The unannounced demand surge never overloaded any UPF even at 4× arrivals across a whole zone, because standing sessions dominate. It contributes a guaranteed zero, which makes the headline conservative but leaves that scenario genuinely untested.
  • The surprise-after-maintenance case still has a bad tail. Pre-drain loses in 30.5% of those pairs and the worst are severe. MPC reduces this to 6.6% with a worst case near zero. If that exposure is unacceptable, MPC is the configuration to take, not pre-drain.
  • Gains are measured against a capacity-proportional static controller. That baseline is already capacity-aware and reacts once an event lands. The advantage shown here comes entirely from acting before it lands.
